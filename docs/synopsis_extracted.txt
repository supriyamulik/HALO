HALO: A Hallucination-Aware Retrieval and Verification Framework for AI-Assisted Legal Research
(Nyaya Sahayak)
1. Introduction
HALO (Hallucination-Aware Legal Output) is an AI-assisted legal research and workflow platform designed to help lawyers search judgments, find relevant legal provisions, get case summaries, and answer legal research questions without depending on a single AI model to answer everything from memory. In its product/user-facing form, the system is also referred to as Nyaya Sahayak. HALO is the name used for the underlying research framework and technical architecture.
Instead of trusting an AI model blindly, the system first searches real legal sources (judgments, Acts, sections) to find actual evidence, and only then lets the AI generate an answer using that evidence. After the answer is generated, every claim and citation in it is checked again to make sure it's actually supported by the retrieved evidence. If something isn't supported, it gets flagged instead of being shown to the lawyer as fact.
At the core of the system is a Query & Verification Pipeline, which acts as the central controller. It takes the lawyer's question, decides what needs to be searched, retrieves the right legal documents, ranks them by relevance, generates a grounded answer, and finally verifies every part of that answer before sending it back.
Each major component of the system — search, ranking, answer generation, and verification — works as an independent, modular service, making the system easier to test, scale, and improve piece by piece.
The system uses Qdrant as a shared vector database for storing embeddings of legal documents, and it uses an LLM (either a cloud model like OpenAI/Gemini, or a local model run through Ollama) for generating answers.
To support day-to-day legal practice, the platform also includes basic case-workflow features like a dashboard, hearing-date tracking, task management, and document storage.
The primary objective of HALO is to give lawyers a legal research tool that is fast and easy to use like a chatbot, but trustworthy like a proper legal database — because every answer is backed by real, checkable evidence.
Naming Convention
This project uses two names deliberately, for two different audiences:
HALO — the research/technical name, used in the synopsis title, the paper, and technical discussions of the retrieval and verification architecture.
Nyaya Sahayak — the product/user-facing name, used when describing the system to lawyers, in demos, and in general conversation.
Both names refer to the same underlying system.
2. Problem Statement
General-purpose LLMs are increasingly used for legal research, but they generate answers primarily from pretrained knowledge rather than from verified, current legal sources. This creates a serious risk in the legal domain: models can fabricate case names, misstate statutory provisions, cite judgments that do not exist, or apply outdated law without indicating that the position has changed.
Existing legal-tech search tools such as keyword-based judgment portals are reliable but limited — they require the lawyer to already know the right terms, sections, or case names, and they do not synthesize an answer across multiple sources. Conversely, LLM-based assistants can synthesize fluent answers but do not guarantee that the underlying facts, sections, or citations are accurate or that they currently reflect Indian law.
Building a trustworthy legal research assistant therefore raises several open problems:
How should a legal query be understood and mapped onto a common retrieval representation?
How can keyword search and semantic search be combined so that both precise statutory citations and conceptually similar judgments are retrieved?
How should the retrieved evidence be reranked so that the most relevant Acts, sections, and judgments reach the LLM?
How can an LLM-generated legal claim be checked against the retrieved evidence, rather than trusted outright?
How can the system verify that a cited judgment or provision actually exists in the authoritative corpus?
How should unsupported or unverifiable claims be flagged or withheld from the lawyer?
How can retrieval quality and answer trustworthiness be measured and compared across different retrieval and verification strategies?
Therefore, there is a need for a legal research system that treats authoritative legal sources as evidence, combines keyword and semantic retrieval, and verifies LLM-generated claims and citations before presenting an answer, rather than relying on an LLM's unverified output. HALO addresses this gap by combining hybrid legal retrieval with an explicit claim-and-citation verification stage.
3. Domain Case Study: Pooja Ramesh Singh v. Jammu and Kashmir Bank Ltd.
Citation & Bench
2026 INSC 668 | Supreme Court of India | Bench: Justices Pamidighantam Sri Narasimha and Alok Aradhe | Decided: 2 July 2026
Background
The case arose from insolvency proceedings under Section 7 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016. Jammu and Kashmir Bank had initiated proceedings against Essel Infraprojects Ltd. (as corporate guarantor) over a loan default of ₹87.43 crore. The NCLT admitted the application and the NCLAT upheld it on appeal.
The Tribunal (NCLT) relied on six purported Supreme Court precedents to support its reasoning — all six were found, on independent verification by the Supreme Court, to be fabricated in some way: some citations did not exist at all, some pointed to the wrong case, and some were correctly cited but the quoted paragraph did not exist in the actual judgment. The bank's own counsel denied citing these cases, indicating the Tribunal itself likely introduced them through its own AI-assisted research — and the fabrication went undetected through a full round of appellate review at the NCLAT.
What the Supreme Court held
A decision resting on fake or hallucinated material is “no decision in the eyes of the law” and amounts to a subversion of the rule of law.
This holds true even if the hallucinated material had only an indirect bearing on the outcome — even “an iota” of fake material is enough to require the decision be set aside.
Courts must adopt a zero-tolerance approach to producing, citing, or using AI-generated precedents without verification.
Citing unverified AI-generated case law is professional misconduct on the part of an advocate, a judge relying on such material is a serious lapse.
The Bar Council of India was directed to constitute a committee and frame guiding principles and disciplinary consequences on this issue.
The Court clarified that the ruling does not restrict the legitimate use of AI in legal work — only the presentation or reliance on unverified fake material as if it were genuine precedent.
The NCLT and NCLAT orders were set aside, and the original Section 7 application was restored for fresh adjudication. The judgment also references a parallel UK case (Anthony Malcolm Cork & Anor v. Mark Smith, [2026] EWHC 1199 (Ch)), where a law firm submitted a fabricated AI-generated statutory quote in a court filing — showing this is not an isolated or India-specific failure mode.
Relevance to HALO
This case moves the project's problem statement from a hypothetical risk to a documented, judicially recognized failure with real institutional consequences. It directly motivates three design requirements for the system: citation verification must check existence, citation-number correctness, and passage-level support (not existence alone),verification must be exhaustive across every claim rather than sampled, reflecting the zero-tolerance judicial standard and the system should be usable by, and protect, institutional users (tribunals, judicial staff) as well as practising lawyers, since the fabrication in this case likely originated on the adjudicating side.
4. Objectives
The objectives below are derived both from the general problem of AI hallucination in legal research, and specifically from the failure modes identified in the Pooja Ramesh Singh case study above.
To design a legal research assistant for lawyers, tribunal staff, and legal researchers, supporting English-language legal queries.
To build a hybrid retrieval pipeline combining BM25 keyword search with semantic (vector) search over authoritative Indian legal sources — Supreme Court and High Court judgments, and Central/State legislation from India Code.
To ingest and structure a verifiable legal knowledge base, including case metadata (case name, citation number, court, date, paragraph numbers) alongside full judgment text, so citations can be checked for structural correctness, not just existence.
To implement a reranking stage (cross-encoder) ensuring the most relevant retrieved evidence, not just the most keyword-similar, reaches the answer-generation stage.
To implement a Legal RAG pipeline in which the LLM generates answers grounded in retrieved evidence, with each claim traceable back to a specific source passage at generation time.
To design a three-tier claim-and-citation verification module checking, for every citation: whether the cited case/section exists in the corpus, whether the citation number correctly corresponds to the case name and paragraph, and whether the quoted or paraphrased text is actually supported by the retrieved passage.
To perform exhaustive, per-answer claim verification — checking every claim rather than a sampled subset — and to flag an answer if any claim is unsupported, reflecting the zero-tolerance standard established by Indian courts.
To generate a per-claim verification status and an overall confidence score for every answer, so the lawyer can see which parts of the response are evidence-backed before relying on them.
To provide supporting case-workflow features — dashboard, hearing-date tracking, task management, document management, and draft generation — making the system usable in everyday legal practice.
To evaluate the system empirically by comparing LLM-only, vector-only RAG, hybrid RAG, and hybrid RAG with three-tier verification, measuring retrieval precision/recall, citation accuracy, unsupported-claim rate, hallucination rate, answer correctness, and response latency.
To validate the system's design against the documented real-world failure case (Pooja Ramesh Singh v. Jammu and Kashmir Bank Ltd., 2026 INSC 668), by constructing test scenarios that reproduce the specific citation-fabrication patterns identified by the Supreme Court, and confirming the verification module correctly flags them.
